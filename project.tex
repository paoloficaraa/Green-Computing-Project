\section{Introduction \& Motivation}
% ============================================================
Clinical machine learning runs inside healthcare IT every day: hospitals
retrain risk scores each night and re-tune them whenever coding practices drift.
Each fit looks cheap in isolation, but across cohorts, seeds, and hospitals
those fits form a standing electrical load with a measurable carbon
counterpart. We measure how much of that load diagnosis requires, and how much
comes from default hyperparameters and interpreted-language overhead.

We frame quality with the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC),
\begin{equation}
\text{MCC}=\frac{TP\cdot TN-FP\cdot FN}
{\sqrt{(TP{+}FP)(TP{+}FN)(TN{+}FP)(TN{+}FN)}}\in[-1,+1],
\label{eq:mcc}
\end{equation}
because accuracy collapses on imbalanced EHR cohorts. In our sepsis cohort
barely one patient in ten deteriorates (\S\ref{sec:testbed}): a trivial
majority classifier scores $\sim$90\% accuracy while learning nothing. MCC
couples all four confusion-matrix cells, penalises majority-class collapse,
and stays invariant under label swapping. An energy saving that destroyed MCC
would waste clinical performance for power, so every figure below pairs joules
with MCC.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Like-for-like benchmark:} three pipelines (baseline Python, regularised Python, native Julia) over five EHR cohorts with identical 100$\times$ stratified 70/30 splits.
\item \textbf{Physical explanation:} how tree depth, DVFS boost states, and memory traffic produce the observed savings.
\item \textbf{Per-cohort analysis:} where variance filtering helps (oncology) and where it harms (acute sepsis).
\item \textbf{Clinical extrapolation:} what the savings mean for a regional hospital network.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Three-layer software architecture}
All pipelines share a three-layer design. The \textbf{orchestration layer}
fixes the experimental protocol: 100 stratified 70/30 hold-out splits (seeds
$0$--$99$), identical per split across pipelines, totalling 500 fits per
pipeline. The \textbf{processing and validation layer} (pandas/scikit-learn in
Python; DataFrames/MLJ in Julia) splits the data, filters variance, trains,
and scores MCC. The \textbf{native execution layer} is where the pipelines
diverge: CPython with \code{RandomForestClassifier} versus Julia compiled
through LLVM with \code{DecisionTree.jl}. We ran one pipeline at a time on an
otherwise idle workstation, with a cool-down between runs; we reused the same
seed sequence for all three, so each pipeline faced the same 500 train/test
problems.

The three contenders are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Baseline Python} (\code{script1.py}): $T{=}100$ trees, unconstrained depth, all features, single-threaded.
\item \textbf{Optimised Python} (\code{script2.py}): $T{=}60$, \code{max\_depth{=}12}, \code{min\_samples\_leaf{=}5}, \code{min\_samples\_split{=}10}, top-60\% variance filtering, \code{n\_jobs{=}1}. We chose these values through empirical trials that compared candidate setups for quality per joule, then froze them across all five cohorts.
\item \textbf{Native Julia} (\code{script3/}): matching hyperparameters, LLVM-compiled, with shared-memory threading (\code{Threads.@threads}) over the 100 splits.
\end{itemize}
We threaded the two runtimes differently (one thread in Python, many in Julia), so read the Python--Julia gap as language efficiency plus parallelism strategy.

To attribute the optimised configuration, we also ran a minimal one-factor
ablation campaign with the same five cohorts, 100 stratified splits, and
single-threaded Python protocol. Each variant changed one lever relative to
the baseline: maximum depth, feature filtering, or forest size. Table~\ref{tab:ablation}
reports the resulting MCC means and the exploratory energy measurements.

\subsection{Energy telemetry and carbon conversion}
We ran all experiments on an Intel Core i7-9700K (8C/8T, 3.60\,GHz), an NVIDIA
RTX~5060 (idle during CPU-only training), and 16\,GB DDR4 under Windows~11.
We read physical energy from CPU hardware counters (Intel RAPL via the Windows
Energy Meter Interface) through CodeCarbon, using the same counters for Python
and Julia; Julia timings include one-off compilation. We converted energy to
Scope~2 carbon with the certified Lombardy grid factor
$I=330.72\,\mathrm{gCO_{2}e/kWh}$, i.e.\ $m_{\mathrm{CO_{2}e}}=E\cdot I$.

\section{Algorithmic Levers \& Hardware Mechanics}
% ============================================================
We pull four levers to cut cycles and hold clocks down:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Depth bounds against DVFS boost.} Under sustained load DVFS raises voltage and clock, and power grows with $V^{2}f$. Deep, unbalanced trees hold hot turbo states and raise junction temperature, which keeps the governor in high-voltage boost. We capped maximum depth at 12 and required at least 5 samples per leaf and 10 per split, and package power dropped to a far lower level (53.96\,W down to 27.05\,W in Python).
\item \textbf{Variance filtering against the memory bus.} We computed variances on the training split and applied the selected columns to the test split, so no test information leaks into selection. A Random Forest tests $\sqrt{M}$ candidate features per node; keeping the top 60\% of columns by variance shrinks the candidate set by $1-\sqrt{0.60}\approx22.5\%$ per node, which removes over a fifth of memory-bus transfers across all 500 fits for a single linear pre-pass.
\item \textbf{Ensemble sizing.} Error variance flattens before $T{=}100$, so capping the forest at 60 trees discards about 40\% of induction work with no measurable MCC loss.
\item \textbf{Language-matched parallelism.} The Python GIL forces scikit-learn into multiprocessing, and array serialisation over the bus exceeds any parallel gain on small tables, so we ran Python single-threaded. Julia shares one table across threads, so parallelising over splits copies almost nothing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{CPU power decomposition}
Our CodeCarbon totals count an idle GPU drawing $\sim$19\,W over PCIe, which
training leaves idle. We isolated the CPU package ($E_{\mathrm{CPU}}\approx
P_{\mathrm{CPU}}\cdot t$) to find the mechanism: the baseline sustains
53.96\,W for 73.22\,s (1.097\,Wh); the optimised run draws 27.05\,W
($-49.9$\%) for 0.321\,Wh ($-70.7$\%); Julia draws 34.92\,W across 8 saturated
cores but finishes in 30.89\,s for 0.300\,Wh ($-72.7$\%). Julia runs hotter
but greener: parallelism raises power and collapses time, and the shorter time
decides the total.

\subsection{Accounting for tuning energy}
The three ablation runs consumed $0.536+0.573+0.333=1.441$\,Wh on the
exploratory laptop. We retain this one-time cost in the repository as
\code{emissions\_ablation\_*.csv}, but do not merge it into the headline
workstation measurement. The deployable optimised Python configuration saves
$1.738-1.005=0.733$\,Wh per 500-fit campaign relative to baseline. Under the
network scenario in Section~\ref{sec:extrapolation}, this corresponds to
approximately $0.733\times50\times10\times365=133.8$\,kWh/year. The
exploratory campaign therefore amortises in about $1.441/0.733=1.97$
equivalent benchmark campaigns; a final physical payback claim requires a
hardware-matched ablation rerun.

\subsection{Predictive quality and the biomedical trade-off}
Quality survives the energy cut except in sepsis (Fig.~\ref{fig:quality}, Table~\ref{tab:mcc}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Oncology: MCC rises.} Neuroblastoma moves from 0.462 to 0.524 and brain tumor from 0.794 to 0.821, because low-variance genomic columns offered trees chance splits.
\item \textbf{Sepsis: MCC falls.} Acute detection drops from 0.531 to 0.416--0.431: life-threatening cytokine storms and leukocyte spikes appear in few patients, so their cohort-wide variance stays low and the filter discards the signals intensivists need.
\end{itemize}
Colorectal mortality stays flat across pipelines, and the near-deterministic depression/heart-failure cohort stays above 0.99 in all three pipelines. The Pareto frontier (Fig.~\ref{fig:quality}b) runs almost vertical: each step from baseline through optimised Python to Julia costs under 0.005 MCC while halving energy.

\section{Real-World Clinical Extrapolation}
\label{sec:extrapolation}
% ============================================================
A single benchmark saves $1.738-0.920=0.818$\,Wh per 500 fits. Scale that to
a regional network of 50 centres, each retraining 10 diagnostic models each
night with our 500-fit validation protocol. The nightly saving is
$50\times10\times0.818\,\mathrm{Wh}\approx409$\,Wh; over a year this compounds
to \textbf{$\approx$149\,kWh} and, at the Lombardy factor,
\textbf{$\approx$49\,kg\,CO$_{2}$e} avoided, alongside $\sim$2{,}150\,h of
freed compute time. Routine hyperparameter search multiplies every fit by
$\sim$5, lifting the saving above \textbf{700\,kWh/year} and
\textbf{250\,kg\,CO$_{2}$e/year}. That matches the annual electricity use of
dozens of households, removed by leaner defaults and a compiled runtime, with
no loss of diagnostic fidelity outside sepsis. At institutional scale, green
defaults compound into budget- and carbon-relevant quantities.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion \& Practical Guidelines}
% ============================================================
We draw three lessons for green healthcare AI:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Regularise by default.} $T{=}60$, bounded depth, and variance filtering recover over 40\% of energy at negligible quality cost.
\item \textbf{Match parallelism to the language.} Single-threaded Python avoids serialisation overhead on small tables, while Julia's shared-memory threading turns cores into net energy savings.
\item \textbf{Filter features with clinical oversight.} Variance thresholding purifies oncology panels but endangers sepsis detection, so allow-list known biomarkers before any unsupervised cut.
\end{itemize}
We cut energy by removing work rather than adding hardware: leaner algorithms preserve cohorts and minority signals, and scale from a workstation benchmark to hundreds of kilograms of avoided CO$_{2}$e across a hospital network.
